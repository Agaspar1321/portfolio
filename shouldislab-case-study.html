ShouldISlab

A calculator that tells you whether paying to grade a trading card is worth it. Halfway through, I found that 28% of my data provider's sales were filed under the wrong card, so I replaced it.

01 · The problem

Grading a card costs a fee, plus selling fees when you sell it. Most grading calculators only show what you'd make if the card comes back a perfect 10. That number is mostly fiction. On a 1999 Base Charizard, about 0.5% of submissions get a 10 and 58% grade 6 or lower.

ShouldISlab computes expected value instead. Each grade's sold price is weighted by how often that grade actually happens, fees come off, and the result is compared with what the raw card sells for today.

02 · Architecture

The browser never talks to a data provider directly. An Express server holds the API keys, caches responses, and runs the verdict math, so the page only gets back a finished answer.

03 · Technical decisions

Cache around a rate limit

The price API allows about 10 requests every 35 seconds, and one verdict can take 7 calls. Without a cache, the second search gets throttled. I added an in-memory TTL cache with lifetimes matched to how fast each kind of data changes: 10 minutes for searches, 6 hours for prices, 24 hours for population. If a 429 still comes back, the server fails fast and the page shows a retry countdown instead of hanging.

Median, not mean

A few huge sales were dragging the average price about 65% high, so I switched to the median. Later I found the new provider's own "comp price" field is a mean, so I ignore it and compute the median myself from the raw sales.

Don't trust small samples

A gem rate measured on 12 graded copies shouldn't look as certain as one measured on 100,000. Every rate comes with a 95% Wilson score interval, and prices with too few sales are flagged instead of shown as fact.

Empty, never zero

When a grade has no recent sales, I fill it from the provider's estimate only if that estimate is based on at least two real grades of the same card. Otherwise the page says "no recent sales." A $0 there would count as a real price and drag the expected value down.

04 · The challenge: changing the API

The first version pulled prices from CardSight. Before building more on it, I tested the data on 10 real cards. I re-read every seller's listing title and checked whether each sale was filed under the right card and grade.

It wasn't. CardSight lumped variants of a card together. For Charizard #4, the regular card, the rare Shadowless and 1st Edition printings, and a card from a different set all went into one "PSA 5" price. About three quarters of its records were also asking prices, not completed sales.

That changed real answers. A base Luka Dončić rookie should say "don't grade" and the Silver version should say "grade it." CardSight pooled them, so it gave the wrong answer for both.

I moved to CardHedge for prices and added GemRate for population data. The switch had a trap of its own. The one-call "all prices" endpoint looked convenient but returns only the last sale per grade, which broke price ordering 23% of the time. I used the slower per-grade comps endpoint instead. The extra calls are why the cache matters.

The browser only calls my own /api routes, so swapping providers was a server change.
